# Growth Loops vs. Funnels: Why Compounding Beats Acquisition

**Guest:** Marcus Odell, Growth Lead at a collaboration software company

Lenny: You've been vocal that funnels are the wrong mental model for growth teams in 2026. Why?

Marcus: A funnel treats growth as a linear pipe — you pour traffic in the top, some percentage trickles out the bottom as customers, and to grow you either widen the top or improve conversion at each stage. That's fine for a one-time campaign, but it doesn't compound. A loop is different: the output of the loop becomes the input of the next cycle. A user invites a collaborator, that collaborator creates content, that content gets shared and pulls in a third user, and the system feeds itself without you spending more on acquisition each time.

Lenny: Walk me through what a real growth loop looks like end to end.

Marcus: Take a document-sharing loop. A user creates a doc, shares it with a colleague, the colleague opens it without an account and sees a "sign up to comment" prompt, some percentage sign up, and now that new user creates their own docs and shares them further. The loop input is "documents created," the loop output is "new users who create documents," and the loop closes when output feeds back into input. The growth rate of a loop is a function of two things: how many people you reach per cycle, and what fraction convert. Improve either one and the whole system compounds, not just the metric you touched.

Lenny: Why do most companies default to funnel thinking even when a loop is available to them?

Marcus: Because funnels are easier to measure and easier to attribute in a dashboard. Paid acquisition gives you a clean CAC number every marketer understands. A loop's payback is fuzzier and shows up over months, not days, so it's harder to defend in a budget meeting. The teams that win at loops are usually the ones with a CEO or VP willing to be patient with a metric that looks unimpressive for the first two quarters and then bends upward sharply.

Lenny: What's the most common mistake teams make when they try to build a loop on purpose?

Marcus: They build a viral mechanic — referral codes, invite bonuses — without checking whether the core product experience naturally produces sharing behavior. If sharing has to be bribed with a discount, you have an incentive, not a loop. The best loops are ones where sharing is a byproduct of getting value from the product, not a side quest you have to convince the user to do. Notion's loop works because sharing a doc is literally how you use the product with a team; the acquisition is a side effect of normal usage, not a favor the user does for you.

Lenny: How do you decide which loop to invest in when there are multiple candidates?

Marcus: I look at loop velocity — how fast one cycle completes — and loop strength, meaning what fraction of participants actually re-trigger the loop. A content loop where SEO content takes six months to rank is slow but can be very strong once it compounds. A viral invite loop can cycle in hours but often decays fast as the addressable network gets saturated. I map a few candidate loops on those two axes and invest in whichever has the best combination of speed and durability for where the company is right now, not which one is trendiest.

Lenny: Any parting advice for a PM trying to convince their org to think in loops?

Marcus: Stop reporting growth as a funnel conversion rate in every meeting. Start reporting it as a loop: here's the input, here's the output, here's the cycle time, here's the k-factor. Once leadership sees growth as a system with feedback instead of a pipe with leaks, the conversations change from "how do we spend more" to "how do we make the loop tighter," which is a much more durable place to compete from.
