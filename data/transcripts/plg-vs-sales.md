# Product-Led Growth vs. Sales-Led Growth: Choosing Your Motion

**Guest:** Grace Bonham, CRO at a workflow automation startup

Lenny: PLG has been the buzzword for a decade now. Is it actually the right model for most B2B startups, or has the pendulum overcorrected?

Grace: It's overcorrected. PLG works exceptionally well when the buyer and the user are the same person, or close to it, and when the product delivers value in minutes without heavy configuration. It works poorly when you're selling into a committee — procurement, IT security, a budget owner, and the actual user are four different people with four different concerns, and no amount of a slick self-serve trial replaces the trust-building a human sales conversation does for that kind of purchase.

Lenny: How do you decide, early on, which motion fits your product?

Grace: I ask three questions. Can a single user get meaningful value alone, without needing coworkers or an admin to configure anything first? Is the average contract value low enough that a human sales cycle would cost more than the deal is worth? And does the buying decision typically involve security or compliance review regardless of price? If the answers are yes, no additional friction needed, and no security review required, you're a strong PLG candidate. If any of those flips, you probably need sales involved somewhere in the motion.

Lenny: A lot of companies try to run both motions simultaneously. Does that work?

Grace: It can work, but only if you're deliberate about where the handoff happens, and most companies aren't. The failure mode I see constantly is sales reps chasing every self-serve signup regardless of usage signal, which annoys small customers who wanted to stay self-serve and wastes sales capacity that should be reserved for accounts actually showing expansion signals. The companies that do dual-motion well have a clear, usage-based trigger — team size crossing a threshold, or a specific high-value feature being used — that hands an account from product-led to sales-assisted, rather than sales cherry-picking based on gut feel.

Lenny: What's the biggest cultural tension between PLG and sales-led teams inside the same company?

Grace: Attribution and ego, honestly. A PLG team wants credit for growth driven by product changes; a sales team wants credit for closed revenue, and when an account came in through self-serve and got closed with sales help, both teams have a legitimate claim. I've seen this tension quietly sabotage roadmap prioritization more than any technical disagreement, because each side starts building metrics to make their own contribution look bigger instead of aligning on what's actually good for the customer and the business.

Lenny: If a company is currently pure sales-led and wants to add a PLG motion, where do they start?

Grace: Don't start by building a self-serve signup flow — start by finding the smallest slice of your product that a single user could get value from without any of the setup your enterprise deployment normally requires. Ship that as a free or trial product first, prove people activate on it without a sales rep in the room, and only then build the broader self-serve funnel around it. Companies that build the funnel before proving the standalone value slice end up with a polished signup flow leading into a product that still requires a human to configure it, which is the worst of both worlds.

Lenny: Any signal that a company picked the wrong motion for their product?

Grace: Persistently low self-serve activation despite good top-of-funnel traffic, paired with sales reps saying deals only close after a demo call regardless of what happened in the trial. That combination usually means the product needs a guided, human-assisted first experience more than it needs a better onboarding checklist — the motion mismatch is masquerading as an onboarding problem.
