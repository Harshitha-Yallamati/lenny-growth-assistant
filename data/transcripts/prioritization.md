# Prioritization Frameworks for Overwhelmed PMs

**Guest:** Owen Vasquez, Group PM at an enterprise HR platform

Lenny: Every PM has a favorite prioritization framework — RICE, ICE, Kano. Do any of them actually work, or is it theater?

Owen: They work as a forcing function for conversation, not as a scoring oracle. The value of RICE isn't the final number it spits out — it's that reach, impact, confidence, and effort each got argued about explicitly by the team instead of staying implicit in someone's gut feel. The failure mode is treating the score as objective truth and stopping the conversation once a spreadsheet says feature A beats feature B by 1.3 points, when the inputs were half-guesses to begin with.

Lenny: What's wrong with just prioritizing based on what the loudest customer or the CEO is asking for?

Owen: Nothing is inherently wrong with weighing a big customer's ask or the CEO's instinct — the mistake is doing it silently, disguised as objective prioritization. If the CEO's opinion is going to override the framework anyway, say so openly, note it as a strategic bet rather than a data-driven pick, and move on. The teams that get cynical about prioritization frameworks are usually the ones where the framework was theater covering for a decision that was already made for other reasons.

Lenny: How do you prioritize when you genuinely don't have good data yet — a new product area, for instance?

Owen: I switch from a scoring framework to an explicit hypothesis structure: state the belief, state what evidence would confirm or kill it, and state the cheapest way to get that evidence within a week. In a low-data environment, ranking ten ideas by fabricated confidence scores is worse than picking the one hypothesis that's cheapest to test and most likely to be wrong in an informative way, because information itself is what you're actually short on, not features.

Lenny: What's a prioritization mistake you see repeatedly from PMs who are otherwise strong?

Owen: Prioritizing based on effort estimates from engineering that were given as rough gut checks in a hallway, then treated as precise inputs in a spreadsheet formula three weeks later. Effort estimates decay fast as more is learned about a feature, and a framework that locks in a stale effort number produces a stale, misleading priority order. I re-check the effort input any time a roadmap review happens, not just at the moment the framework was first run.

Lenny: How do you handle competing priorities from different stakeholders — sales wants feature A, support wants bug fixes, leadership wants a strategic bet?

Owen: I make the tradeoff explicit and visible rather than trying to satisfy all three quietly with a compromise roadmap that under-delivers on everything. I'll show leadership: here's what we ship if we optimize for sales-driven revenue this quarter, here's what we ship if we optimize for reducing support load, here's what we ship for the strategic bet — pick one as the dominant lens for this quarter and treat the other two as secondary. Trying to do all three at 33% effort each usually produces worse outcomes on every dimension than committing to one dominant lens per quarter.

Lenny: If a new PM could only take away one thing about prioritization from this conversation, what should it be?

Owen: The framework is a communication tool, not a math problem. Its entire value is making the reasoning behind a decision visible and arguable by the team, so pick whichever framework your team will actually use consistently and argue about honestly, rather than searching for the theoretically perfect framework and never settling on one at all.
