# Pricing and Packaging: The Most Underrated Growth Lever

**Guest:** Sofia Nkemelu, Monetization Lead at a B2B analytics company

Lenny: Why do you think pricing gets so much less attention from product teams than acquisition or activation?

Sofia: Because it feels risky and irreversible in a way a landing page test doesn't. Nobody gets fired for A/B testing a headline; plenty of people are scared to touch pricing because a mistake is visible to every paying customer at once. But that avoidance is expensive — I've seen a single packaging change move revenue more than a quarter of aggressive acquisition spend, because pricing affects every single customer in the funnel simultaneously, not just new ones.

Lenny: What's the first thing you look at when a company asks you to fix their pricing?

Sofia: Whether the pricing metric — the thing you charge per unit of — actually scales with the value the customer receives. If you charge per seat but your product's value comes from data volume, you're capping your own revenue on your happiest, highest-usage customers while your metric punishes teams for adding users, which discourages the exact expansion behavior you want. Getting the pricing metric right matters more than getting the price points right.

Lenny: How do you approach the classic three-tier "good, better, best" structure?

Sofia: The tiers should each target a genuinely different buyer, not just a bigger version of the same buyer. If your middle tier is just "more of tier one," you're leaving the decision entirely to price sensitivity, which trains customers to always pick the cheapest option. Instead, design each tier around a different job to be done — self-serve individual, team collaboration, and org-wide governance are three different buyers with three different willingness-to-pay ceilings, not three sizes of the same thing.

Lenny: What about the anchor tier — is a deliberately unattractive top tier a real technique or a myth?

Sofia: It's real, but it only works if the top tier is genuinely valuable to someone, not a decoy. A well-designed "Enterprise" tier that includes SSO, dedicated support, and custom contracts will pull a meaningful fraction of buyers who need those things and will also make the middle tier look reasonably priced by comparison. A fake decoy tier nobody would ever buy erodes trust the moment a prospect asks a sales rep about it and gets a vague answer.

Lenny: How often should a company revisit pricing?

Sofia: I'd say a light review every two quarters and a serious structural review once a year, tied to when your ideal customer profile shifts. The trigger isn't a calendar date, it's evidence — sales reps discounting heavily and consistently, a specific segment churning citing price, or a competitor repositioning around a metric you don't currently support. Any one of those is a stronger signal than "it's been a year."

Lenny: What's the biggest pricing mistake you see repeatedly?

Sofia: Grandfathering every existing customer into old pricing forever out of fear of backlash. It quietly caps revenue for years and creates a two-tiered customer base that's operationally painful to support. The companies that handle this well give existing customers a long, generous, clearly communicated transition window rather than a permanent exemption — customers respect a fair transition far more than founders expect, and permanent grandfathering costs far more than the backlash it was meant to avoid.
