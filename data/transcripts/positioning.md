# Positioning and Messaging When Everyone Looks the Same

**Guest:** Naomi Callahan, Head of Product Marketing at a devtools company

Lenny: Devtools is famously crowded. How do you approach positioning when five competitors could copy-paste your homepage headline?

Naomi: I stop trying to win on features, because a feature comparison table is a race to the bottom that ends in everyone claiming the same five bullet points. Instead I position around the specific alternative the customer is actually using today — usually a worse tool, a manual process, or a competitor with an obvious weakness — and make the headline about the contrast with that specific alternative, not a generic superlative like "the best way to build software," which means nothing because everyone says it.

Lenny: How do you figure out what that "actual alternative" is instead of guessing?

Naomi: Sales call transcripts and lost-deal interviews, read literally, not summarized. Reps and marketers tend to paraphrase what customers said into cleaner language, and the paraphrasing strips out the exact words that would make great messaging. When a prospect says "we're just duct-taping three scripts together right now," that phrase, close to verbatim, is a better headline than anything a positioning workshop will produce, because it's proven language a real buyer used to describe their pain.

Lenny: What's the biggest positioning mistake you see technical founders make?

Naomi: Leading with architecture instead of outcome. A founder will describe their product as "a distributed event-streaming platform with exactly-once semantics" when the buyer's actual question is "will this stop our data pipeline from silently dropping records at 2am." The technical description is accurate and completely fails to connect, because it answers a question the buyer isn't consciously asking yet. Outcome-first messaging, with the technical depth available one click deeper for the buyers who do care, converts far better than leading with the architecture.

Lenny: How often should positioning change as a company matures?

Naomi: Meaningfully, at each stage where your ideal customer changes. Early on, you're often positioning against "doing nothing" or a manual process, because your buyer hasn't adopted any tool yet. Once a category matures and buyers already have some tool, your positioning has to shift to contrast against that incumbent tool specifically, which is a different message entirely. Keeping early-stage "replace nothing" messaging once your market has moved to "replace the incumbent" is a common reason growth stalls even as the product keeps improving.

Lenny: How do you test whether new positioning is actually working, versus just sounding good internally?

Naomi: I distrust internal reactions entirely and watch for two external signals: whether sales reps start using the new language unprompted in their own calls within a few weeks, and whether inbound leads start describing their problem back to you using your new phrasing instead of generic industry terms. If reps quietly revert to their old pitch, or leads never adopt the new language, the positioning didn't land no matter how good it sounded in the workshop that produced it.

Lenny: Any advice for a founder about to write their own homepage headline this week?

Naomi: Write down the exact sentence a happy customer used to describe why they switched to you, unedited, and see how close your current headline is to that sentence. Almost every time, the customer's own words beat whatever the marketing team workshopped, because the customer's words already survived contact with reality and yours haven't yet.
