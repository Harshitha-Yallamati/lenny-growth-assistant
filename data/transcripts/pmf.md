# Finding Product-Market Fit Before You Run Out of Runway

**Guest:** Priya Ramanathan, former Head of Product at a Series B marketplace startup

Lenny: Let's start with the question everyone asks but nobody agrees on the answer to. How do you actually know when you've found product-market fit?

Priya: The honest answer is you feel it before you can prove it with a dashboard. The clearest signal I've seen across three companies is organic pull — customers referring other customers without being asked, support tickets shifting from "how do I use this" to "when will you build X," and retention curves that flatten instead of decaying to zero. If your growth is entirely a function of how much you spend on ads, you don't have product-market fit yet, you have paid acquisition that happens to convert.

Lenny: A lot of founders confuse a good launch week with product-market fit. What's the tell that it's fake?

Priya: Vanity retention. You'll see a spike of signups from a launch, a nice looking week-one retention number, and then week four comes and the cohort has evaporated. Real product-market fit shows up in the second and third month retention curve, not the first week. I tell teams: don't trust any retention number you haven't seen survive at least two cohorts past the initial spike.

Lenny: You mentioned the Sean Ellis test — "how would you feel if you could no longer use this product" — do you still use it?

Priya: I use it as one input, not the verdict. If more than 40% say "very disappointed," that's a good signal, but I've seen teams hit that number with a passionate niche of 200 users while the broader market shrugs. The test tells you fit exists somewhere; it doesn't tell you the market is big enough to build a company on. You have to pair it with qualitative interviews asking what they'd use instead if you disappeared tomorrow. If the honest answer is "a spreadsheet" or "nothing, I'd just live without it," that's a very different signal than "I'd have to piece together three other tools."

Lenny: How long should a team give themselves before concluding they don't have fit and need to pivot?

Priya: There's no universal number, but I'd be worried if you're eighteen months in with a stable product and you still can't point to a specific segment that's obsessed with you. The mistake I see most often isn't pivoting too early — it's refusing to pivot because the team has fallen in love with the solution instead of the problem. The founders who found fit fastest were the ones who could describe their user's pain in the user's own words, verbatim from interviews, not in product-speak.

Lenny: What's one thing teams get wrong operationally while searching for fit?

Priya: They scale the team before they scale the signal. I've watched companies hire ten salespeople to "accelerate growth" when the real problem was that only 8% of trial users reached the aha moment. Fixing that activation gap would have done more for the business than any hire. Product-market fit work is disproportionately about narrowing — narrowing the ICP, narrowing the core use case, narrowing the onboarding path — before you're ready to widen anything.

Lenny: If you had to pick one metric to obsess over during the search for fit, what would it be?

Priya: Retention at week eight, segmented by acquisition channel and use case. Not signups, not even week-one activation. Week eight retention, segmented, tells you which slice of your market actually can't live without you. Once you find that slice, your job changes from searching to expanding — same problem, bigger radius, and that's a completely different kind of company-building.
