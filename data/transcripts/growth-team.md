# Building Your First Growth Team

**Guest:** Ibrahim Kader, first Growth hire turned VP at a two-sided marketplace

Lenny: When should a company make its first dedicated growth hire, versus just having product and marketing handle it?

Ibrahim: The signal isn't headcount or revenue, it's whether you already have a validated core loop or funnel that just needs optimization, versus still needing fundamental product-market fit work. A growth hire before product-market fit usually gets misused running acquisition experiments on a product that isn't ready to retain the users those experiments bring in. Bring in growth once you have something worth compounding, not before.

Lenny: What should that first growth hire actually look like — generalist or specialist?

Ibrahim: Generalist, almost always. Your first growth person needs to be comfortable writing SQL to find a retention insight in the morning, briefing a designer on an onboarding test at noon, and reviewing an email sequence in the afternoon. Specialists are a luxury for later, once you know which lever — activation, retention, referral, monetization — actually matters most for your business. Hiring a paid acquisition specialist as your first growth hire, when your real problem is activation, is a very common and expensive mistake.

Lenny: How do you structure a growth team once it's past the first hire — embedded in product, or a separate pod?

Ibrahim: I've run both, and a separate pod with a clear, narrow charter beats embedding growth people into existing product teams as a part-time responsibility. Growth work requires a different cadence — smaller, faster experiments, comfort with a high failure rate, and metrics reviewed weekly instead of quarterly. Product teams optimizing for a big roadmap item don't naturally protect time for that cadence, so growth initiatives quietly starve unless they have dedicated headcount and a leader who reports growth metrics directly to the CEO.

Lenny: What's a red flag that a growth team has drifted from real impact into busywork?

Ibrahim: A growing backlog of "wins" that are all statistically significant but individually tiny, with no story about how they compound. If your growth team reports fifteen A/B test wins a quarter and the company's actual growth rate hasn't moved, something's wrong — either the tests are optimizing a metric disconnected from revenue, or the wins are real but too small in absolute terms to matter, and the team has optimized for a high win rate instead of high-impact swings.

Lenny: How do you keep a growth team from just running the same funnel-optimization playbook forever?

Ibrahim: Force a quarterly review of the loop or funnel model itself, not just the experiments running inside it. Teams get comfortable optimizing conversion rate on an existing signup flow long after the bigger opportunity has moved elsewhere — say, to lifecycle emails or a referral mechanic that doesn't exist yet. I schedule a standing "are we optimizing the right system" conversation separate from the weekly experiment review, specifically to catch this drift before a whole year goes by optimizing a flow that's already near its ceiling.

Lenny: What's the most important trait you look for when hiring growth people now?

Ibrahim: Comfort being wrong in public, repeatedly. Growth work has a low hit rate by design — most experiments don't move the needle — and the people who thrive treat a failed test as one data point closer to the real answer, not a personal failure. The best growth people I've hired share results, including flat and negative ones, with the same energy as their wins, because that's what actually builds a team's collective intuition over time.
